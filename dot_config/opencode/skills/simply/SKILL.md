---

Explain like the reader is a curious kid encountering this topic for the first time. Use ordinary words and a natural tone, not baby talk. Don't assume they know the building blocks, but don't reteach what they've already shown they understand.

## Build the explanation

1. **Give the simplest true answer first.** In a sentence or two, say what the thing is, what it does, or what the passage means. Say why it matters to the reader's question. Don't start with a glossary or a history lesson.
2. **Make it concrete.** Use one small example, preferably from the reader's situation. If it's a process, walk through one instance in order: what starts it, what each part does, and what happens as a result. Connect the example back to the general idea so the reader can recognize it in another situation. A short labeled diagram can help if the relationships are easier to see than read.
3. **Teach the useful names as you go.** Explain a new idea in familiar words, then give the exact term the reader will see in a document, tool, or conversation. Define necessary jargon at first use; don't define it with more unexplained jargon. If two things are easy to confuse, show what each does in the same example.
4. **Add detail in layers.** Stop once the reader has enough to answer their question. Don't hide a condition that changes the answer, and don't turn a guess about the reader's situation into a fact. For a rewrite, preserve the original meaning, including qualifications and uncertainty.

## When the first explanation doesn't click

- Don't just shorten or repeat it. Try a different example, show a before-and-after, or explain the missing prerequisite first. Ask one focused question only if you can't tell what needs explaining.
- Use an analogy only if it's more familiar than the topic and helps explain how it works. Say which parts match and where the comparison stops working. Return to the real thing rather than making the analogy do all the work.
- For a quick definition, a sentence or two may be enough. Don't force a template, an analogy, or a quiz. If the reader wants practice, ask one why or what-if question about a slightly different example instead of testing vocabulary.

## Basis

- [CDC plain-language guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/health-literacy/php/develop-materials/plain-language.html): lead with the main message, use familiar words, and break information into manageable pieces.
- [IES practice guide, recommendations 2–4 and 7](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/1/Published): show worked examples, pair useful visuals with words, connect concrete examples to abstract ideas, and use explanatory questions when practicing.
- [Glynn's Teaching With Analogies](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ed373306): connect the relevant features explicitly and point out where the analogy breaks down.
